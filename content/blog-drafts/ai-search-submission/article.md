# Can You Submit Your Brand to AI Search? What That Viral Link Actually Is

You have probably seen the reel. Someone shares a screenshot of a form and says: submit your brand here so AI search can find you. We followed the link to see where it goes. The link is real. It just is not what the caption made it sound like. The form is an application for retailers to hand their product catalogue to a shopping feature, not a place where a service business signs up to be recommended.

That distinction is the whole post. The advice traveled further than its actual purpose. Here is what the form really is, what the other platforms offer, and the discovery work that genuinely moves a service brand.

## What the OpenAI form actually is

The old OpenAI Product Discovery URL now redirects to [OpenAI's merchant page](https://chatgpt.com/merchants/). That page is an application for retailers to share product catalogue data so their items can show up in shopping results. It is not a general brand-submission form.

You can tell by what it asks for. The form wants a merchant website, product categories, feed readiness, and a unique SKU count. That is inventory language. It is built for ChatGPT Shopping, which OpenAI says is currently live in the United States. OpenAI's current merchant page also notes that Shopify and Etsy catalogues are already integrated, so those sellers do not need a separate application, and that merchants can appear through crawling without a feed, though a feed gives more control.

Now picture a professional-services firm. Say you run a consultancy, an agency, or a law firm. You sell expertise and outcomes, not retail products with SKUs. There is nothing in that form for you to fill in honestly. Submitting it would mean inventing a product catalogue that does not exist, to enter a shopping pipeline you do not belong in. So a service business should not submit this form. It is the right link for the wrong kind of business.

## The other platforms have the same boundary

The other major platforms run similar systems, and the same line holds. These are commerce lanes for retailers with products to sell, not a route for a general service brand.

- **Google and Gemini: Google Merchant Center.** Product feeds, structured product data, images, price, and availability. A retail product lane. Google says Merchant Center data can support discovery in AI Mode, AI Overviews, and Gemini shopping experiences.
- **Microsoft and Copilot: Microsoft Merchant Center.** Product feeds and UCP-ready commerce attributes. A product-discovery and checkout lane. Current UCP feed support is U.S.-focused, and Merchant Center feeds can inform organic Copilot product results, while paid Copilot ads are a separate lane.
- **Perplexity: the Perplexity Merchant Program.** Retail product catalogues, introduced for large merchants and shopping experiences.
- **Anthropic and Claude:** no public merchant or general service-brand submission route in the official material reviewed. Claude uses Claude-SearchBot for search indexing and Claude-User for user-directed retrieval. The relevant action here is crawler access and strong public web content, not a merchant application.

Two things are worth saying plainly. Product feeds are a direct data route into shopping experiences, not a route for submitting a general service brand. And even for merchants, submitting a feed does not guarantee that every product will appear or be recommended. A commerce feed and an organic citation are different surfaces. Geographic access varies.

So the honest summary is this. In the official material reviewed, no major answer engine offered a public form where a service business could submit itself for organic recommendations. The absence is not an oversight you can shortcut. It is the point. Organic recommendations are earned through the public web, not filed through an intake form.

## The real service-brand discovery stack

There is no form. There is work. This is the part that actually earns a mention when someone asks an AI for a recommendation. Seven steps, in order.

### 1. Let the relevant crawlers reach the site

An AI can only recommend what it can read. Audit your robots.txt, page-level robots directives, and any CDN or firewall blocks that quietly turn bots away.

Know the crawlers, because they are not all the same. For OpenAI, allow **OAI-SearchBot** for ChatGPT Search eligibility; **GPTBot** is the separate training crawler, and **ChatGPT-User** handles user-triggered visits. For Anthropic, allow **Claude-SearchBot** for search discovery and **Claude-User** for user-directed retrieval; **ClaudeBot** is the separate model-development crawler. For Perplexity, allow **PerplexityBot**, while **Perplexity-User** handles user requests. For Google and Microsoft, keep important pages accessible to **Googlebot** and **Bingbot**. Allowing a bot does not guarantee inclusion. It only removes one possible technical block.

### 2. Make important pages indexable and fresh

Verify your pages in Google Search Console and Bing Webmaster Tools. Submit and maintain an XML sitemap with accurate canonical URLs and lastmod values. Inspect priority URLs after you publish or make a substantial update. Use IndexNow where it is supported.

Then fix the quiet failures: accidental noindex tags, canonical conflicts, redirect chains, soft 404s, and pages that depend on scripts a crawler cannot run.

### 3. Publish pages that answer one clear service question

Give each priority service and customer situation its own page. State who the service is for, what problem it addresses, the location or jurisdiction, and the next step. Put the important information in readable text, not locked inside images, video, or accordions that fail to render.

Use descriptive headings and concise answer blocks that can stand alone when quoted. Support your claims with primary sources, dates, named authors or reviewers, and real evidence. Keep the page current, and let visible update dates reflect meaningful changes rather than a cron job. Microsoft's Bing Webmaster guidance recommends clear headings, tables, and FAQ sections, supporting claims with evidence, and keeping content fresh for AI citation. Google says its normal helpful-content and technical SEO practices apply to its AI features.

### 4. Build one consistent entity across the web

Publish the same verified facts everywhere they belong. Canonical business name and legal name. Website and contact page. Address, phone, and service area. Logo and official social profiles. Founding and credential information where it is publicly verifiable. Google Business Profile and Bing Places. Relevant professional or regulatory registry profiles.

Connect it with Organization, LocalBusiness, WebSite, Article, and appropriate page-level schema, and make sure the structured data matches the visible content. If you want to know why so much schema does nothing, our companion piece [Is Your Schema Actually Helping?](https://www.thedotcreative.co/blog/is-your-schema-actually-helping) walks through it.

### 5. Give systems independent sources to corroborate

An answer engine trusts a brand more when the claim shows up in places the brand does not control. Professional or regulatory listings. High-quality local and industry directories. Client reviews on real platforms. Earned media, interviews, and expert contributions. Partner, association, and event pages. Original research or data others can cite.

This is not a guarantee an AI system will recommend the brand. It gives retrieval systems more independent public evidence to work from.

### 6. Monitor citations, not just rankings

Track your ChatGPT referrals; OpenAI says referral URLs include `utm_source=chatgpt.com`. Review Bing Webmaster Tools' AI Performance report. Watch for Google's generative AI performance reporting as it rolls out in Search Console.

Keep a fixed test set of prompts: branded, non-branded, service, location, and problem-based. Record the sources each answer cites, not only whether your name appears. Re-test after meaningful changes, and do not treat one prompt on one day as a stable ranking.

### 7. Avoid fake shortcuts

Most of the bad advice lives here, so it is worth naming.

- Do not submit a service firm to a merchant form with invented SKUs.
- Do not add Product schema to a professional service just to sneak into a shopping pipeline.
- Do not publish schema that is invisible, outdated, or inconsistent with the page.
- Do not sell llms.txt as a required ranking switch. Google explicitly says no special AI text file or special AI schema is required for AI Overviews or AI Mode, and OpenAI's publisher guidance focuses on crawler access.
- Do not block every bot with "AI" in its name without separating the training crawlers from the search and user-retrieval crawlers.
- Do not promise inclusion, ranking, or recommendations. No one can honestly guarantee those.

One accuracy note that keeps the record straight: Google says there are no additional technical requirements, special AI files, or special schema for AI Overviews and AI Mode. A page simply needs to be indexed and eligible for a Search snippet.

## Before you follow the next viral link

A real link can still be the wrong tactic. Check what the destination is built to accept before you spend an afternoon filling it in. The merchant form is real. It is also for merchants. For a service brand, the path to being recommended runs through the seven steps above, not through an intake screen.
